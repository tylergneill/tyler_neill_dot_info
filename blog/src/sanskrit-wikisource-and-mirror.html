A set of questions came up recently on the Indology mailing list about Sanskrit Wikisource — what is it, who made it, and how useful is it? I've spent some time working this out, so here's what I know.

Wikisource is part of the Wikimedia family — same foundation as Wikipedia — and like its siblings, it is a radically open, crowdsourced project with public revision history and no single creator. The Sanskrit subdomain has been used by motivated individuals to work on and share material they might not have otherwise, which is something to be genuinely thankful for. On multiple occasions, I've found very interesting things there, like full texts of both Gaṅgeśa's Tattvacintāmaṇi and Śrīharṣa's Naiṣadhīyacarita. But Wikisource in general was simply not built to host e-text collections, and the Sanskrit space in particular lacks curatorial vision. As a result, it can be very hard to plumb the depths of its contained materials — a fact ironically reinforced by the Wikisource logo itself.

Did you know that Sanskrit Wikisource has hundreds of megabytes of text data? If you’ve ever tried to browse it, did you succeed? No? You're not alone.

Access Problems

The best way to navigate Sanskrit Wikisource is not clear from its home page.

First, not everyone is comfortable with an all-Sanskrit, all-Devanagari interface. Second, it's a cluttered mess. If you look past the less useful stuff, the वर्गसर्वस्वम् section offers some links to category pages, but clicking them gives you only a narrow view of one category at a time.

Pro tip: There's a separate https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/वर्गः:वर्गसर्वस्वम् page a few wiki-clicks away, if you know what you're looking for. There, you find a dynamic dropdown tree structure that is already much better for browsing.

As for the categorical breakdown itself, it’s mostly unobjectionable, but as is always the case, there can be a learning curve if you come from a different taxonomic perspective. And there is undoubtedly mess here, too. By convention on Wikisource, categories are supposed to form a single, cohesive tree covering everything, but some things are misplaced (e.g. धर्मशास्त्रम् at the top-level), some are unhelpfully separated due to naming differences (ज्योतिष्यम् 9.9 MB and ज्यौतिषम् 1.6 MB), some are orphaned (ज्यौतिषम् is within वेदाङ्गानि, but ज्योतिष्यम् is free-floating), and many items lack categories altogether.

And what is an "item" deserving of categorization anyway? It's quite subtle, actually. Abstraction to a "text" level is purely manual and not at all well expressed by the wiki structure. One Wikisource editor may choose to put an entire large text on a single page. Another may choose to spread it across dozens or hundreds of pages connected by nested tables of contents. Sometimes, instead of using a table of contents page, an editor simply labels all parts of a work with the same Category, e.g., गरुडपुराण-आचारकाण्डः. Furthermore, an editor may sometimes wish to rename one or more pages, but because it's hard to chase down and fix all inbound connections, one option they have is to just set up redirect pages, adding further layers of navigational complexity. Users are at the mercy of all this curatorial diversity when trying to browse.

Granted, there are other, non-categorical access options, as well: a search bar, and an alphabetical index (e.g., फ). But I find both of these to be myopically restrictive in their own ways, too. My bet is that, even after trying all of the above, a typical user may still be shrugging their shoulders about whether this collection has material that might be useful to them. After all the work that went in, by so many people, that's a shame.

A Better Interface

Fortunately, it's possible to create a better way in, even without modifying Wikisource. Based on my understanding of the collection's structure, I made an alternate interface to the same exact data that I personally find much more user-friendly.

https://tylergneill.github.io/sanskrit-wikisource-mirror

What this alternate interface gives you is: 1) cleaner browsing and search in a single-page display with a sidebar; 2) no Wikimedia artifacts (things; 3) transliteration options; 4) more intuitive abstraction to tbe "text" level; and 5) clearer stats, specifically page and text counts, plus size and last-updated info.

The way I did it: 1) download the full data; 2) process it to calculate relationships and counts; 3) boil that info down to a single file; and then 4) build a simple single-page app on top of that file, which GitHub Pages easily hosts for free.

This web app only organizes and presents links to the source data on Sanskrit Wikisource itself. It does not re-host the data, since the collection is demonstrably still living and growing. The mirror's last-updated info for each item clearly shows this, as does Wikisource's own “सद्यपरिवर्तनानि,” i.e., Recent Changes page. Like Pāṇḍitya's use of Pandit Project, this mirror starts from a data snapshot, builds functionality on top, and points back to the source. That does mean that it needs to be periodically refreshed to stay current (pipeline here in Git).

What the snapshot numbers from Summer 2026 reveal: Sanskrit Wikisource holds about 390mb of plain-text content in 2,200 texts across roughly 22,000 individual web pages. For comparison, GRETIL is around 350mb before controlling for duplication, roughly 175mb after (see previous post). In short, it is definitely huge, and it's very interesting.

It's Actually More Complicated...

The mirror is almost too user-friendly. Under the hood, it's doing a lot to compensate for Sanskrit Wikisource's structural problems. I'll explain a little of that here. For more detail, see the About page.

TL;DR: Wikisource relies on a surprising amount of manual work to ensure navigability, and unsurprisingly then, there are a lot of issues.

Inherent Structural Issues

Diverse ways of using the structure: Some works may be spread across dozens or hundreds of sub-pages, while others are confined to just one. Some are typed in by hand, some are created using OCR, and some are imported from other text collections. Pages relate to each other only through connections a human sets up by hand, either a "breadcrumb" parent/child link (naming a subpage "Parent/Child") or a Category tag applied directly to a page. Nothing computes these relationships automatically, and nothing enforces that they're applied consistently. Things get lost

Curation Issues

As a fully crowdsourced project, Sanskrit Wikisource has no central oversight and a lot of diffusion of responsibility — anyone can come along and post things, like a bulletin board, and no one necessarily comes along afterward to clean it up or keep it organized. That can still be fine, as long as we're clear that this is s a bulletin board and not a curated collection. no metadata! given navigability problems, and the anonymous nature of decentralized, crowdsourced work, of course duplications do occur, with nothing to tell users which version of a given Sanskrit work may be preferable for a given purpose. For example, my entry point into this exploration was a desire to digitize Śrīharṣa's Naiṣadhīyacarita, one of the traditional five mahākāvyas, for HANSEL, learned that sanskritsahitya.org already had a version derived from Wikisource, and then struggled to find it there, ultimately finding multiple versions and the host of other issues discussed here. One day of trying to understand how Wikisource even works later, I was convinced the right move was to clarify the collection for others before doing anything else. Hopefully this post saves you that day.

Enter the Interpretive Pipeline

Since I want to keep the mirror fresh, I've built it to rely on a pipeline that's easy to rerun regularly. That pipeline can compensate for the above issues, interpreting data in order to present it in more user-friendly ways.

Change Over Time

Nobody likes reading wikipedia revision history. Similarly, most Sanskritists are not going to read Wikisource revision history. If you know where to look, Wikisource has some automated tooling pages that can give you an idea. But for most users, there's basically no practical changelog.

Suggested Improvements

The mirror's About page also shows both how Sanskrit Wikisource has changed over time and how we can improve it going forward. But you don't really need to care about that to profit from the main-page browsing and search interface.

Structural Problems, Quantified

Building this mirror meant an audit pipeline to surface where that structure breaks down in practice, and the numbers are worth sitting with: 60 categories are orphaned from the tree entirely; 16 are wrongly filed under more than one parent; 36 are referenced but were never actually created, leaving red links; and 200 Mainspace pages look like missed breadcrumb subpages — titled with a space where a "/" should be, so the parent/child link never got made. (Full tables, again, on the About page.)

The upshot is exactly what I ran into browsing by hand: anything not correctly wired into this structure becomes findable only via full-text search, or not at all unless you already know its exact title. Duplication is a related symptom of the same wiki-format inertia — pages are hard to delete or move once created, so old and new versions of a text just coexist, with nothing to indicate which is preferable. And provenance is a wrinkle I hadn't expected: at least 226 pages appear to be imported wholesale from other online Sanskrit collections (sanskritdocuments.org, muktalib7.com, GRETIL, and others), almost always without a note to that effect on the page itself.

How to Proceed

Without Modifying Wikisource

As mentioned, this mirror functions as an epiphenomenal stop-gap while several of the above issues can be gradually worked out.

Another strategy people sometimes use in this sort of a situation is to just copy all of a project's data and do their own thing with it somewhere else, changing it as they see fit, and presenting it as the better version. In some situations, that's fine, and Wikisource's own default is Creative Commons BY-SA 4.0 (in Sanskrit: “भिन्नोल्लेखः यावत् न भवेत्, तावत् CC BY-SA 4.0 इत्यत्र उल्लेखो भवति ।”) But I hope to have shown in this post why that is NOT a good idea. The collection is so riddled with issues, it's very liable to be misunderstood. AND it's still being edited all the time, so any copy-pasted snapshot will be misleadingly stale before long.

Instead, my opinion is that we as a scholarly community should approach items one by one, study them carefully, and where possible, improve them and transplant them onto platforms built for proper scholarly curation. sanskritsahitya.org's Naiṣadhīyacaritam is an example of just such a transplant.

With Modifying Wikisource

Of course, we can also improve Sanskrit Wikisource itself. I voiced a lot of dissatisfaction with the home page, for example, but I could also try to change it. While making the mirror, I came across a half-dozen or so systematic issues that warrant a systematic cleanup effort. My pipeline code includes an "audit" pathway which generates specific suggestions, surfaced on the About page's Data Quality section.

My experience with editing Wikipedia, however, is that this kind of collaborative, anonymous work can be socially tricky. I'd rather not bank on being able to make whatever changes I think are best. Maybe I'm wrong about some things. Maybe people who have been contributing to the site for years will see me as an annoying upstart trying to rock the bock — and not a native-speaker of Sanskrit to boot! Finally, it also just takes a lot of time to do cleanup in individual cases, for relatively little reward.

That's why my first order of business was to make a mirror, so that I and others can start making better use of the collection as a whole, now. I'd also like to work on the gradual cleanup job, time permitting, but now others can hopefully see the issues and be motivated to get involved, too.

Coming Back to the Surface

As intriguing as I found this Sanskrit Wikisource case, it's actually just the beginning. There are several other similarly underestimated text collections out there. My plan is to extend the alternate-interface treatment to them, and to consolidate these interfaces into a single portal, which I will maintain as an additional Kalpataru Grove project named Sāgarasaṅgama, "Meeting of the Oceans." As these collections become better understood, their metadata can then be ingested into SETI, and their prosopographical aspects represented in Pandit Projec. Once the latter two are done, the items can finally be visualized in Pāṇḍitya. That's the big-picture side of the plan.

At the finer-grained scale, I'll also plan to adopt and transplant individual items of high potential value into HANSEL, and I hope others do the same on that or similar platforms.

I'd love to hear your thoughts on any of this, please be in touch.
