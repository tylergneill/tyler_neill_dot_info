Recap

In the previous quarterly update I imagined I would start to write up the Q2 Skrutable meter id work, and relatedly, continue the Mahāsubhāṣitasaṃgraha digitization and benchmarking work. I also hoped to give SETI some love. But some other things were more pressing.

For one, I helped Muktabodha go live with its new digital library (see announcement on Indology), and thanks to a generous outside collaboraton, there are a few dozen additional items on the way. HANSEL also crawled along, but the slow pace belies some underlying advancements. At the intersection of these two corpus-building efforts, I’ve been creating some experimental new tools that should greatly improve the digitization and proofreading process, for these or other projects, not unlike this curious toolkit. More on those tools when they mature a bit.

The main thing I spent time on, though, was the hoped-for evolution of my Sanskrit Wikisource “Atlas” into the larger Sāgarasaṅgama project, now released: blog post and Indology announcent. To reiterate the idea: There are several super-collections of online Sanskrit e-texts that most of us have bumped into at one time or another, especially Sanskrit Documents, Sanskrit Wikisource, Jain Quantum (a particular snapshot of Jain eLibrary), and E-bharatisampat. But in my experience, students and scholars don’t engage with these collections in a sustained way because of access issues. Either they don’t know about the collection, or they know about it but don’t believe it contains much, or they are aware of the large size but still have trouble getting at or using the actual material for one reason or another. So I’ve been making bespoke “Atlas” interfaces to facilitate access to individual collections, plus a “Sāgarasaṅgama” (“meeting of the oceans”) meta-interface to compare across collections and search their metadata in one go. In other words: finding aids and a meta-finding aid.

This gives us, for the first time, a striking historical summary of the volume of searchable Sanskrit text online, now with GRETIL for context.

I hope these new tools can facilitate an ongoing conversation on where we should be looking to find the large-scale digital material we need to understand Sanskrit language and literature. That conversation should of course include detailed consideration of e-text quality.

Re-centering the Vision on Visibility

ISCLS 9 is coming up in January, to be held at Deccan College in Pune, and I plan on going and presenting a paper. In it, I’ll be sharing my thoughts on 1) how we’ve previously understood this question of e-text data online, 2) how the visibility problem has impacted us and my approach to solving it, and 3) what tools I’m using to facilitate future additions to this vast and growing online library.

I’m more and more convinced that visibility is a major and underestimated problem — and the yawning gap now clear between GRETIL and the larger collections only strengthens the point. Why spend unnecessary time hunting for or even recreating this material if it is already online in a usable form? And if it already exists, why shouldn’t even beginning students have instant access to the full range of literature in a form they can easily page through or search? Otherwise, there’s such much unnecessary struggle and wasted effort. The combination of SETI, Sāgarasaṅgama, and Pāṇḍitya are all coming at this from slightly different angles. Before long, we’ll have comprehensive, disambiguated insight into the thousands of top-tier Sanskrit works that history has deemed worthy of printing as editions and then digitizing. This curated bibliography metadata will be publicly open to all, and there will be multiple ways to interact with it — traditional search and browsing interfaces, visualizations, and the raw tabular data — depending on one’s needs and preferences.

Once I solve this problem of metadata visibility over the next few years, I’m excited to get on to creating textual data of other sorts, too. New e-texts are one piece, and for that I’m using HANSEL (and Muktabodha, too, although I’m not the primary curator in that case). Creating annotation data, like what Oliver painstakingly did with DCS over decades, would also be a way I’d enjoy sitting down and reading through lots of text at length. And then there’s model training and inference services, like what Sebastian and co. have produced on the basis of the DCS data. But for now, metadata is the focus.

For all of these tasks, I’m becoming relatively sure now that AI is indispensable for helping us arrive there in good time. And not just a script here or there. With coordinated swarms, even large data tasks requiring language expertise at multiple levels can be broken down and handled in parallel at astonishing speed, with accuracy that rivals any human team. Of course there are limits; I won’t be trusting AIs to read handwritten manuscripts anytime soon. But for the kind of work I’m most interested in, I do believe 90-95% can be automated. The trick lies in understanding how to unleash this kind of power in responsible ways, with each step appropriately audited before proceeding to the next. No one wants a “slop cannon.”

Technology is accelerating, and even that acceleration is increasing. (In physics, a positive third derivative like this is literally known as “jerk.”) When applied with good judgment to the bibliographic and other data problems of our field, I have no doubt that AI can greatly support and advance traditional humanities understanding. That’s what I’m after.

Cool-down and Consolidation

In Q4 of this year, I’m resisting the urge to race ahead toward more digitization (#3 above) and other new shiny objects. Instead, I’ll be for focusing on upgrading existing projects that are key for solving the visibility problem (#2 above), which especially includes SETI. In the coming weeks, look for a significant upgrade to that as it and Sāgarasaṅgama begin to converge on the same data. Pāṇḍitya will also be getting some attention, so if you have ideas for how to improve that interface, now would be a good time. I’ll also try to clear my Skrutable and HANSEL backlogs.

The agent swarms are standing by, infinitely ready for delegated duties. Breathe in, breathe out. Combobulate.

I think then mid-next year, once I've gathered my energies, it will be time for a big and fun digitization push — both cleaning up and upgrading old material, and putting entirely new stuff online. That will also be a good time to explore tech-supported analytic reading, to build out more annotation data. I’m really looking forward to it — especially the actual reading part!
