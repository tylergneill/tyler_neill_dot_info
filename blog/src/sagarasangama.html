Background

Most of my formal Sanskrit training happened at Cornell, Harvard, Vienna, and Leipzig, and most of my close colleagues over the years have been based in English- or German-speaking countries. From what I've seen in those places, the story of searchable, academic-grade Sanskrit e-texts online — what I'm most interested in these days — has largely been what can now be found via my own SETI inventory, or in Sebastian's Dharmamitra database, or on Harry Spier's SALSa aggregation platform: some one to two thousand texts, consisting 90+% of some combination of GRETIL and Muktabodha and DCS, supplemented by additional, smaller open collections like SARIT and UT Austin Dharmaśāstra — all collections guided by university philologists. These collections are open in the sense that not only students and scholars but also builders can take them in their entireties and do new creative things with them.

Of secondary importance over those same years — I think I can again speak for my academic community of origin — was a small number of yet other collections, sometimes much larger: things like Jain eLibrary and Sanskrit Documents. I would check these, too, but only after exhausting other possibilities, including private Dropbox and Google Drive collections I happened to be invited to share. In my view, what kept these collections at the margins of academic work was not so much the quality of the material, which is often excellent, but how it is structured and released; if the collection is meant to be read on the website, one text at a time, rather than downloaded as a whole and searched locally, Western-trained scholars are going to be less interested.

Anyway, the above is the past from my perspective. Around 2020, I ducked into a tunnel to write my dissertation, graduate, start a family, and work for a few years as a software engineer. In January of last year, when I re-engaged with Sanskrit and the problem of digital data online — including HANSEL as part of my answer to the demise of GRETIL — I noticed some new things making noise on that personal radar of mine. Things like Sanskrit Wikisource.

My previous post about that collection claimed that there are actually multiple "iceberg" collections out there, containing huge amounts of Sanskrit e-text material, which are sufficiently difficult to understand and use that people — or people I know at least — are underutilizing or even disregarding them. The next step was clear, and now I've taken it: the same "Atlas" treatment I gave to Sanskrit Wikisource has now been extended to Sanskrit Documents and E-bharatisampat. The details of that work (see more below) has removed all doubt: the landscape really has changed, and for the better. These collections still do not empower effective mass download and search, but that is their prerogative, and we must respect it for the time being.

Even with these two new Atlases, there still remain two major limiting factors: 1) awareness of these collections and proper appreciation of what they are, and 2) a practical alternative to having to search them one at a time. In my experience, these collections' SEO is not sufficient that people will just Google their way across the text they need. And rather than try to convince people to use all these individual websites or the respective Atlases, it occurred to me I could take one more step and bring them together myself in a synoptic way, to help get at the really big picture.

Sāgarasaṅgama

If each of these mega-collections is an "ocean," requiring a bespoke Atlas to properly navigate and understand, then a system organizing each collection's metadata into a common form and enabling comparison and search across them constitutes a "meeting of the oceans." Et voilà.

http://sagarasangama.info/

By formalizing key measurements in the form of a "contract" between the individual Atlases and the supervening Saṅgama, we finally get to something like apples-to-apples comparison of scope, plus federated search across titles and authors. Still nothing is mirrored; all links drive web traffic back to the source sites.

This effort is a celebration of the grandeur and potential usefulness of each of these collections. In my capacity as a librarian, I've essentially made a "finding aid" for making better use of an important body material. I hope this contribution will increase use of the collections, and I hope the Atlas's "audit" section, which identify and illuminate structural faults, are a temporary report that can be deleted after these faults are addressed.

If nothing else, I want Sāgarasaṅgama to raise awareness of these collections as significant, namely by emphasizing their size and history. In that sense, Sāgarasaṅgama's About page is what's going to turn heads initially.

For a moment, just assume that these figures are correct. (I should and will substantiate them in due course.) And then just sit there with them for a moment. For context, let me rehash some basics of GRETIL. According to its own history page, it started in 2001 and reached its end-of-life in late 2020. Now frozen in time, in its raw form, it is 350 MB of text and 1,800 texts. After you deduplicate for its variant textual forms — analyzed texts, indices, etc. — it stands closer to 175 MB and 1,100 texts. (This comparison should get even clearer in a few months once I give GRETIL the full "atlas" treatment and it properly appears in these same figures, so don't worry.)

The "Collection sizes" table is already quite suggestive, but the "Growth over time" chart tells the really interesting story. In 2016, Sanskrit Wikisource outstripped GRETIL in size. In 2021, E-bharatisampt rocketed past both and has continued climbing at an astounding pace, ostensibly with much greater use of OCR. (The smaller story is Sanskrit Documents, which is largest in terms of number of texts, but those texts tend on average to be small devotional items (mantras, stotras, etc.), so while large, it has still not reached GRETIL proportions, and it has nothing like the topical range of the other three.)

As someone with a stated interest in "academic-grade" e-texts, one should naturally ask: But what about quality? Are these three collections as trustworthy as something like GRETIL? Having worked with them a little bit more now, I can answer: yes and no. For one thing, GRETIL itself has a bunch of pretty junky stuff on it, as we all know: things that are unproofread, extremely partial, super weirdly formatted, etc. etc. So even though we've relied on it so extensively, we can't pretend it's super high quality. And for their own parts, the three "oceans" discussed here (Sanskrit Wikisource, Sanskrit Documents, E-bharatisampat) definitely have some less than perfect material, too. But I am sure now, there is also very solid material, certainly usable for academic purposes, albeit not fully reliable or suitable for citation. If one of these collections has something that is nowhere else on the internet, then we should be able to find that fact out quickly, ideally without having to search each of them individually.

That is why I created Atlases to organize and improve access to them, and why Sāgarasaṅgama brings it all together, including with search over title and author — NOT fulltext search. In theory, one could do fulltext search, too, like Harry's SALSa project. But there are conversations to be had before I go about using these collections' data in that way, and anyway, I don't want even users trusting me with that kind of responsibility just yet. I think it's enough that Sāgarasaṅgama functions as a finding aid, quickly identifying where to look closer, sort of like WorldCat.

So the big shift now is from focusing only on things under the streetlamp — the GRETILs and other familiar non-Indian projects, and of course whatever private collections you or anyone may have access to — toward the big, unruly, intimidating ones that in the last ten years have actually surged ahead in terms of public data that, theoretically, the whole world can use, if only they can understand it. It's time we do just that.

Two new Atlases

Now, a bit as an afterthought...

E-bharatisampat Atlas

Sanskrit Documents Atlas

As a reminder: Each giant and unique collection gets a bespoke interface called an Atlas. The goal is not to mirror the material (i.e., re-host it myself in its entirety as a sort of backup) but only to represent it more clearly, through an alternative front-end overlay, so that users can better appreciate its scope, celebrate it, and make better use of it. Doing this requires diving deep into the structure of these websites and their collections, to make sure I understand what I'm trying to represent, in all its tangled detail, before publishing anything. As a result, each Atlas surfaces key insights about the collection's size and history.

And a confession: In order to ensure that I'm presenting the material correctly, I have to really get intimate with a given collection. What I do is just go ahead and scrape — politely — until I'm confident I've understood the collection, warts and all. Each Atlas has pipeline code that can be re-run, but to make sure that people don't use my code to assail these websites with unwanted traffic, I keep the scraping code private. What is surfaced publicly, however, is the results of a structural "audit," on the respective About page, in the "Data Quality" section. I believe being aggressive in this way is in service of a genuinely good cause: increasing use of these collections.

More Atlases to come

GRETIL? Sure. Jain eLibrary? I'd love to, but we'll see. As for the various little ones, I don't think they each need an Atlas — which seeks to plumb confusing depths with bespoke structure that is too hard for every individual user to learn — but I do have another mechanism in mind: inventory them with SETI, and then give SETI a pseudo-Atlas that allows it to integrate into Saṅgama. Even Harry's SALSa, which is starting to uniquely host some material otherwise found only on a department website (much like GRETIL originally did) can be inventoried at least for its unique holdings — not all that it appropriates from other online collections — through SETI. And eventually, I'd like to expand SETI itself with at least some of the material illuminated by the Atlases, so that we can update Pandit Project accordingly and have Pāṇḍitya work as the one Sanskrit e-text finding tool to rule them all.

(I still love you, Pāṇḍitya! I think you're the most fun to play with 😉 and I'll be back to help you out again soon.)
