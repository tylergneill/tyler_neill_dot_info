Braving the Sanskrit Wikisource Iceberg

July 25, 2026

A set of questions came up recently on the Indology mailing list about Sanskrit Wikisource — what is it, who made it, and how useful is it? As it happens, I had spent time earlier in the year investigating this very thing, and I came up with a prototype solution that addresses some of the obstacles to understanding. As a result of the Indology exchange, I dug in deeper and developed the prototype further, which I'm now ready to share more widely. First thing's first, though. Let's try to answer those basic questions.

What is it?

Wikisource is part of the Wikimedia family — same foundation as Wikipedia — and like its siblings, it is a radically open, crowdsourced project with public revision history and no single creator.

Who made it?

Since 2004, the Sanskrit subdomain of Wikisource has been used by motivated individuals and groups to work on and share Sanskrit e-text material they might not have otherwise without such an easy-to-edit platform. These contributors are noted in the item revision histories, but you have to go looking for them. To take a random example, on the page याज्ञवल्क्यस्मृतिः/आचाराध्यायः/उपोद्घातप्रकरणम्, clicking "इतिहासः दृश्यताम्" shows you the revision history, and then you can click on the specific username Sbblr0803  to learn that this is a person named Abhiram, see what else he has done, etc.

How useful is it?

On multiple occasions, I've found very interesting things there, like first-online postings of full texts of Gaṅgeśa's Tattvacintāmaṇi and Śrīharṣa's Naiṣadhīyacarita. I think it is much more potentially useful than many people realize.

But.

The Wikisource platform has inherent pros and cons for hosting an evolving e-text collection like this, and on balance, I think the cons win out. It's hard enough to keep a sprawling, decentralized project like this on track, but the Sanskrit space in particular seems to lack curatorial vision. There is just a lot of mess, which I'll discuss more below. As a result, it can be very hard to plumb the depths of its contained materials — a fact ironically reinforced by the Wikisource logo itself.

Did you know that Sanskrit Wikisource has hundreds of megabytes of text data? If you’ve ever tried to browse it, did you feel inspired to come back again the next time you need something? No? You're not alone.

Access Problems

The best way to navigate Sanskrit Wikisource is not clear from its home page.

First, not everyone is comfortable with an all-Sanskrit, all-Devanagari interface. Second, it's a cluttered mess. If you look past the less useful stuff, the वर्गसर्वस्वम् section offers some links to category pages, but clicking them gives you only a narrow view of one category at a time.

Pro tip: There's a separate https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/वर्गः:वर्गसर्वस्वम् page a few wiki-clicks away, if you know what you're looking for. There, you find a dynamic dropdown tree structure that is already much better for browsing.

As for the categorical breakdown itself, it’s mostly unobjectionable, but as is always the case, there can be a learning curve if you come from a different taxonomic perspective. And there is undoubtedly mess here, too. By convention on Wikisource, categories are supposed to form a single, cohesive tree covering everything, but some things are misplaced (e.g., धर्मशास्त्रम् at the top-level), some are unhelpfully separated due to naming differences (ज्योतिष्यम् 9.9 MB and ज्यौतिषम् 1.6 MB), some are orphaned (ज्यौतिषम् is within वेदाङ्गानि, but ज्योतिष्यम् is free-floating), and many items lack categories altogether.

And what is an "item" deserving of categorization anyway? It's quite subtle, actually. Abstraction to a "text" level is purely manual on Wikisource and can be done in various ways. One Wikisource editor may choose to put an entire large text on a single page. Another may choose to spread it across dozens or hundreds of pages connected by nested tables of contents. Sometimes, the top-level table of contents page is not labeled with a Category, but the second-tier (e.g., chapter) pages are. Furthermore, an editor may sometimes wish to rename one or more pages, but because it's hard to chase down and fix all inbound connections, one option they have is to just set up redirect pages, adding further layers of navigational complexity. Users are at the mercy of all this curatorial diversity, which may or may not be implemented correctly.

Granted, there are other, non-categorical access options, as well: a search bar, and an alphabetical index (e.g., फ). But I find both of these to be myopically restrictive in their own ways, too. My bet is that, even after trying all of the above, a typical user may still be shrugging their shoulders about whether this collection has material that might be useful to them. After all the work that went in, by so many people, that's a shame.

A Better Interface

Fortunately, it's possible to create a better way in, even without modifying Wikisource. Based on my understanding of the collection's structure, I made an alternate interface to the same exact data that I personally find much more user-friendly.

https://tylergneill.github.io/sanskrit-wikisource-mirror

By understanding and compensating for many of the issues listed above, this alternate interface gives you: 1) cleaner browsing and search in a single-page display with a sidebar; 2) no distracting Wikimedia artifacts (e.g., "निष्कासनाय", "to be deleted"); 3) transliteration options; 4) more intuitive abstraction to tbe "text" level; and 5) clearer stats, specifically page and text counts, plus size and last-updated info.

The way I did it: 1) download the full data; 2) process it to calculate relationships and counts; 3) boil that info down to a single main data file; and then 4) build a simple single-page app on top of that file, which GitHub Pages easily hosts for free.

This web app only organizes and presents links to the source data on Sanskrit Wikisource itself. It does not re-host the data, since the collection is demonstrably still living and growing. The mirror's last-updated info for each item clearly shows this, as does Wikisource's own “सद्यपरिवर्तनानि,” i.e., Recent Changes page. Like Pāṇḍitya's use of Pandit Project, this mirror starts from a data snapshot, builds functionality on top, and points back to the source. That does mean that it needs to be periodically refreshed to stay current (pipeline here in Git).

What the snapshot numbers from Summer 2026 reveal: Sanskrit Wikisource holds over 700 MB of plain-text content (measured in IAST) in something like 2,000–3,000 texts across roughly 26,000 individual web pages. For comparison, GRETIL is around 350 MB before controlling for duplication, roughly 175 MB and 700 unique works after (see previous post). In short, it is definitely huge, and it's very interesting.

It's Actually More Complicated...

The mirror is almost too user-friendly in how it smooths over Wikisource's rough edges, but since I believe that the Wikisource data should be improved in-place, the mirror's build system also includes an audit pipeline which calculates and exposes on the About page a list of structural issues that we can actually fix without too much effort.

Growth over 15+ years

Rather than stop at visualizing only the present snapshot, I also have the mirror system look back in time and compare month-over-month change, giving trend graphs and full detail at monthly, quarterly, or annual granularity. This will also work going forward. You can see for yourself just how much is changing all the time. LINK

This is possible partly because of some diligent archiving efforts, partly because Wikisource itself provides convenient full dumps for certain eras, and partly because the nature of full revision history is that, similar to Git, basically any file from any moment in time can be virtually materialized for comparison. The kind of macro-level analysis described above is not provided by Wikisource. What is more commonly referenced, however, is the micro-level. Namely, every single change to every single item is documented in perpetuity: which user did it, when, and the full diff. Most Sanskritists and scholars will recoil from the very dense technical presentation of this feature, but it's there!

An in-built OCR pipeline

In addition to the option to simply type or copy-paste material in manually, Wikisource also includes its own OCR-based pipeline, called "Proofreading." The process starts with a user uploading a PDF, say. Wikiksource creates a central page for the project, then auto-generates individual pages for each page of the PDF. Page-by-page, Wikisource gets the ball rolling with Tesseract OCR, and then users manually improve the text in a facing-page editing interface. Through a process called "transclusion," the editor can connect a normal page to this gradual proofreading process, such that updates are automatically reflected.

The big advantage of this method of data input is that source images are automatically preserved on Wikimedia Commons and findable from the text page. There are pitfalls: not every editor chooses to transclude properly, and material may remain in an unproofread state for a long time. The mirror does its best to notice deviations from best practices and still surface material in a usable way.

Limited metadata

With the exception of the OCR-based texts mentioned above, when they successfully link to, e.g., a source title page, most Sanskrit e-text content on Wikisource lacks any usable metadata at all over and above revision history. Most importantly, not knowing what edition or editions a given text is based on can make it extremely difficult to use responsibly. Users are left with the implicit claim that, e.g., this just is the Pañcadaśi by Vidyāraṇya. An enterprising researcher may well figure it out by comparing against available printed editions. But why the guessing game?

Better than nothing, Wikisource's default to CC BY-SA 4.0 where not specified otherwise (“भिन्नोल्लेखः यावत् न भवेत्, तावत् इत्यत्र उल्लेखो भवति ।”) at least clarifies terms of use.

Curation Issues

As a fully crowdsourced project, Sanskrit Wikisource has no central oversight and a lot of diffusion of responsibility — anyone can come along and post things, like a bulletin board, and no one necessarily comes along afterward to clean it up or keep it organized. That obviously has the advantage of keeping things moving quickly. We just need to understand what we're working with.

Should be at all surprised, then, that there are categorization issues? page structuring issues? mistakes that leave material nearly invisible? huge variance in data quality? multiple versions of works seemingly unaware of each other?

For example, take my own entry point into this exploration, an interest in a digital version of Śrīharṣa's Naiṣadhīyacarita, one of the traditional five mahākāvyas. Having found it on sanskritsahitya.org, and traced it further to Sanskrit Wikisource, I was surprised to find two distinct versions: this one, which..., and this one, which ... The absurdity of this experience was what led me to starting digging and building, in hopes of sparing both my future self and others similar pain in the future.

Different Ways Forward

Option 1: Mirror

Obviously, this mirror is itself a light-touch approach to mitigating problems with the collection.

Option 2: Duplicate the Data and Don't Look Back

Another way people deal with the difficulties of a collection like this is to take lots or all of the data in the form of a static copy and then just do what they want with it, such as aggregating it with data from other sources. To be sure, this does open up interesting possibilities for federated processing, like search. Examples are Vishvas Vasuki’s raw_etexts repo LINK and associated website LINK, and Harry Spier’s SALSa LINK. Wikisource's default license is Creative Commons BY-SA 4.0 (in Sanskrit: “भिन्नोल्लेखः यावत् न भवेत्, तावत् CC BY-SA 4.0 इत्यत्र उल्लेखो भवति ।”), so legally, this is all kosher, as long as one makes proper acknowledgment.

That all said, I hope to have shown in this post why doing this — especially without further contextualization — is NOT a good idea from a scholarly perspective. The collection is so riddled with issues, it's very liable to be misunderstood. AND it's still being edited all the time, so any copy-pasted snapshot will be misleadingly stale before long.

Aside: Interestingly, in the audit pipeline, I was able to add a separate informational component LINK that shows where Sanskrit Wikisource has itself borrowed from other collections — at least 226 pages (about 5% of the total collection by content size) from sanskritdocuments.org, Muktabodha, GRETIL, and others.

Option 3: Adopt the Best, Clean Up the Rest

Instead, my opinion is that we as a scholarly community should approach items one by one, study them carefully, and where possible, improve them and transplant them onto platforms built for proper scholarly curation. That is what I intend to do in select cases with HANSEL LINK .

AND, of course, we CAN also improve Sanskrit Wikisource itself, either at the top, navigational level, or lower levels like mid- and low-tier categories. For example, I voiced a lot of dissatisfaction with the home page, but I could also try to change it. Similarly, if I see something miscategorized, why not just submit an edit and see what happens? My honest answer: Before having this mirror as a tool, I did not feel very motivated to do this on any sort of scale. Not yet being a part of the editing community myself, but having had certain experiences on Wikipedia, I know that it's possible that my changes will just be reverted. More crucially, though, I didn't have enough perspective to know whether my effort would make a difference.

That's why my first order of business was to make a mirror, so that I and others can start making better use of the collection as a whole, now. But in doing so, I found it was possible to systematically identify, categorize, and surface the finite number of low-hanging-fruit edits that would be needed to fix the site’s major problems. See the About page's Data Quality section. Only now, with this tool in hand, do I feel like it would be a good use of my time to start an improvement campaign. Because now I know the extent of the job — when I’ll be able to say my cleanup is done — and what each step consists in. Also, by making it public, now it doesn’t have to be just me who works on this. Anyone who is interested can immediate get the full perspective and be motivated to pitch in, too.

Coming Back to the Surface

As intriguing as I find this Sanskrit Wikisource case, it's actually just the beginning. There are several other similarly underestimated Sanskrit icebergs out there. My plan is to extend the alternate-interface treatment to them, and to consolidate these interfaces into a single portal, which I will maintain as an additional Kalpataru Grove project named Sāgarasaṅgama, "Meeting of the Oceans." As these collections become better understood, their metadata can then be ingested into SETI, and their prosopographical aspects represented in Pandit Projetc. Once the latter two are done, the items can finally be visualized in Pāṇḍitya. That's my big-picture goal, and I think it's doable in three to five years.
